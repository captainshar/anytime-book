### 8.4 Going to College
- **Homeschool + FAFSA (gap, now covered):** the 2026–27 FSA Handbook, Vol. 1 Ch. 1, says students may self-certify on the FAFSA that they finished homeschooling as defined by state law. Schools may rely on that. If the state issues and requires a homeschool completion credential, the student must have it. **So no diploma does not mean Ability to Benefit only**, which corrects Gemini. The parent-signed transcript problem is a *college admissions* rule, not a federal aid rule. The page sends readers to 8.3 for workarounds. Please check the note reads clearly.
- **Independence explanation** comes from the 2026–27 AVG Ch. 5: provisional independent status, the aid office's options, documented interview allowed, carry-forward at the same school, and the "parents just refuse" case meaning an unsubsidized loan only. The born-before-January-1-2003 cutoff is confirmed in AVG Ch. 2. The list of independence reasons on the page is shortened and says "for example."
- **SwiftStudent is effectively dead.** formswift.com shows "FormSwift has closed its doors as of September 2026." Dropped.
- **TRIO EOCs:** ed.gov blocked fetches. News says 2026 EOC grants were rewritten and only about 55 centers funded. Not listed as a resource. The one-thing-first now sends readers to their community college's aid office instead of an EOC.
- **H.R. 1 / OBBBA:** the page says only that "some federal loan limits changed in July 2026" and that Workforce Pell is starting for short job-training programs. Workforce Pell's dates are confirmed on fsapartners (GENERAL-26-32). The 8–15 week / 150–599 clock-hour detail came from search results, not an ED page, so it's not stated. The loan-limit details came from a college page (govst.edu). Consider whether the full-cost-of-attendance Pell rule matters to this reader. I left it out.
- The "pushy for-profit schools" trap is general advice with no single official source. The FTC and studentaid.gov have pages on this; one could be linked once a browser can load them.

## Hand to Gemini

Paste Block A first, then one of these.

**1. Homeschool records and transcripts for estranged adults (8.3 / 8.4)**

> Topic: Getting or replacing homeschool high school records as an adult when parents won't help. For homeschool graduates whose parents never made, lost, or are withholding a diploma or transcript, find: (a) what each US state lets an adult do — which states keep any homeschool records (notice of intent, portfolio reviews, superintendent letters) and how an adult can request their own; (b) which colleges and community colleges accept a student-written transcript, a GED/HiSET, or college credits instead of a parent-signed transcript, and how to ask; (c) umbrella/cover schools and online programs that keep records, and how to request them (including through Parchment); (d) how federal student aid treats homeschool completion versus college admission rules; (e) legal or advocacy help (for example Coalition for Responsible Home Education, homeschool alumni groups). Cite each state's own education department page where possible. Flag anything that needs a parent signature.

**2. Trade schools, online colleges, and spotting predatory schools (8.4 / 8.5)**

> Topic: Choosing a trade school, certificate program, or online college safely. Find free official tools to check accreditation (U.S. Department of Education DAPIP, CHEA), compare costs and outcomes (College Scorecard), and check if a program qualifies for federal aid, including the new Workforce Pell grants for short job-training programs starting 2026–27 (what programs qualify, how to find them by state). Explain the warning signs of predatory for-profit schools and fake accreditors, where to report them, and what borrower-defense or closed-school loan discharge means. Include free unbiased advising options.

**3. Low-cost, credit-bearing online courses (8.4)**

> Topic: Cheap ways to earn real college credit online. Compare Modern States + CLEP (free courses, exam vouchers; test-center fees), DSST, Study Hall (Crash Course and Arizona State University; current enrollment and credit-conversion prices), ASU Universal Learner Courses, Sophia, Saylor Academy, and community college online classes. For each give current 2026 price, how credit transfers, ID and test requirements, and refund rules. Note which ones federal aid can pay for. Warn about programs whose credits rarely transfer.

**4. Dyslexia and learning-difference supports for adults (8.3 / 1.1)**

> Topic: Free or low-cost help for adults with dyslexia or other learning differences who were never tested. Find free or low-cost adult screening or evaluation (Vocational Rehabilitation, community college disability offices, university clinics), free text-to-speech and reading tools built into phones and computers, accessible books (NLS Talking Books, Bookshare — current adult eligibility and cost, Learning Ally), and GED/HiSET testing accommodations and the paperwork they need. Plain language; flag any that need a diagnosis.

**5. Low-cost computers and home internet (8.1)**

> Topic: Getting a cheap computer and home internet in 2026, after the Affordable Connectivity Program ended. Find national refurbished-computer nonprofits that ship to all states (with ID and income rules), the Lifeline program's current benefit, internet providers' own low-income plans, and how to find local digital-navigator help. Flag which need photo ID or proof of income.

**6. "Correcting what you were taught" (possible new Topic)**

> Topic: Catching up on mainstream science, history, and health after a biased or religious-only education. Find free, neutral, beginner-friendly sources that teach what mainstream schools teach (evolution and earth science, US and world history including civil rights, sex education, basic health), with a gentle tone for people whose beliefs are being challenged. Include courses, textbooks, videos, and communities or therapists experienced with religious-trauma learners. Avoid sources that mock religion.
