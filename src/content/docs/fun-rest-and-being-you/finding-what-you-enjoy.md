Most people find out what they like as kids, by trying things and being allowed to say "I don't like that." If your time was assigned to you, or fun was called a waste or a sin, you missed that practice. You can get it now. This page covers how to try things, how to tell what you like, and where to try things for free.

:::tip[Start here]
Pick one thing you've been curious about, however small: drawing, a kind of music, a walk somewhere new. Do it for 15 minutes this week. Afterward, ask one question: **would I do that again?** Any answer is useful.
:::

## What's going on

Nobody knows ahead of time that they'll love fishing or be bored by chess. They try it, notice how it went, and try something else. Liking things is learned by doing.

That takes three things many readers of this book didn't have: time that was yours, the freedom to choose, and the freedom to stop. Without them, you can reach adulthood good at many things and unsure whether you enjoy any of them.

### Why fun can feel wrong

Maybe you were taught that every hour has to be useful, or that pleasure leads somewhere bad. Then doing something for no reason can bring on guilt or dread. That reaction is old training. It isn't evidence that you're doing something wrong.

It tends to get quieter each time you do the thing and nothing bad happens.

### How to tell if you like something

The signs are often small. Time went by faster than you expected. You thought about it later. You'd do it again even if nobody ever knew.

Two things are easy to mistake for liking. Being good at something is one. Being praised for it is the other. Some people find that what they were made to practice for years is what they enjoy least. Others keep it. Both happen.

A flat first try doesn't mean you have no likes. If you spent years not being asked what you wanted, noticing takes practice too.

### If you're a beginner as an adult

Adult beginner classes exist for almost everything, because adults start new things all the time. Being bad at something at first is how it goes at any age. You don't have to get good at it for it to be worth doing.

### If you like something you were told was bad

Music, dancing, novels, card games, makeup, movies: many groups forbid things that are ordinary in most of the US. People of many faiths, and of none, do them.

What you make of what you were taught is your call. You can try something first and decide what you think of it later. [Knowing Who You Are](/your-mind-and-feelings/knowing-who-you-are/) and [Finding Meaning Again](/your-mind-and-feelings/finding-meaning-again/) go further into that.

## How it works

1. **Make a short list.** Three things is enough. Things you were curious about as a kid. Things you've watched other people do. Anything from the list below.
2. **Pick the smallest, cheapest version.** Borrow before you buy. One class, not a course. A pencil, not an art set.
3. **Do it once, for 15 to 30 minutes.** Set a timer if that helps you stop feeling you owe it more.
4. **Notice.** Afterward, ask: would I do that again? Did the time go fast or slow? Write one line down if you want a record.
5. **Give it another try or two before you decide.** First tries are awkward for everyone. Awkward is different from not liking it.
6. **Keep it or drop it.** Quitting is part of how this works. Most people try many things and keep a few. Nobody is owed an explanation.
7. **Try a different kind of thing next.** If you tried making something, try moving, or going somewhere.

## Things to try

Each of these is free or close to it, and none of them needs another person.

- **Make something.** Draw with a pencil on any paper. If you don't know where to start, make a big scribble and color in the shapes. Cook one thing you've never cooked.
- **Move.** Walk a route you've never walked. Put on one song and move to it with the door closed. Shoot baskets at a public court.
- **Listen.** Play three kinds of music you've never heard. Many libraries lend music and movies.
- **Read.** A novel, a comic, a magazine about cars or cooking or space. Sit in the library and read the first pages of five different things.
- **Write.** Set a timer for 10 minutes and write whatever comes. Nobody has to see it.
- **Grow something.** Put the white ends of green onions in a jar of water on a windowsill. A cup and some dirt work for seeds.
- **Play.** A deck of cards and the rules for solitaire. A jigsaw puzzle from a thrift store (a shop that sells donated, used things cheaply). Some libraries have game nights.
- **Go outside.** Sit in a park for 20 minutes. Watch birds, water, or people.
- **Go look at something.** A museum, a free concert in a park, a high school game. It's ordinary to go alone.

When you want other people in it, [Making Friends](/talking-to-people-and-the-world/making-friends/) covers how to find groups built around an activity.

## Where to get help

### Get ideas

**[Why hobbies are good for your mental health (Mental Health America)](https://mhanational.org/resources/why-hobbies-are-good-for-your-mental-health/)**
A short page from a long-running mental health nonprofit. It covers five kinds of hobby: art, reading, writing, music, and gardening. Each has a few small steps to try today.

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone with internet
- **How to start:** Scroll to one of the five and do its first step. You can also download the page to print.
- **Good to know:** No account needed. One of its music ideas is a community or church choir. Skip any idea that doesn't fit you. The page ends with a link to a mental health quiz. You don't need to take it.

### Go somewhere free

**[Library Finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/CommunityServices/find-libraries.aspx)**
A free tool from the U.S. Department of Labor that shows the public libraries closest to you. Libraries are free to walk into, and you can stay as long as they are open.

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone
- **How to start:** Type in your city or ZIP code and pick the closest library. No internet? Ask anyone nearby where the public library is. Walk in and look around. You don't need a card to be there, and nobody will ask why you came. You can sit and read all day. To find something, ask at the desk: "Where are the magazines?"
- **Good to know:** To borrow things, you need a library card. At the front desk you can say: "I'd like to get a library card. What do I need?" Each library sets its own rules. If you don't have ID or proof of address, ask what else they accept. Also ask if they have a **"Library of Things"** (things like ukuleles, cake pans, games, or sewing machines) or **free museum passes**. Not every library has these.

**[National parks: Plan Your Visit](https://www.nps.gov/planyourvisit/index.htm)**
The National Park Service runs more than 400 parks, trails, and historic places. Most are free to visit.

- **Cost:** Most sites are free. Some charge an entrance fee. The [passes page](https://www.nps.gov/planyourvisit/passes.htm) lists a few free days each year. Those free days are only for US citizens and permanent residents.
- **Who can use it:** Anyone
- **How to start:** Open the page and search for a park by location, activity, or topic.
- **Good to know:** Many national parks are hard to reach without a car. City, county, and state parks are often closer.

**[Museums for All](https://museums4all.org/)**
If you get SNAP (food stamps), more than 1,600 museums let you in for $5 or less. Some are free. They include art museums, science centers, zoos, and gardens.

- **Cost:** $5 or less for up to four people per EBT card. (EBT is the card your SNAP benefits come on.)
- **Who can use it:** People who get SNAP food benefits
- **How to start:** Use the map on the site to find a museum near you. At the ticket desk, show your EBT card and a photo ID. The card is only to show you qualify. It isn't charged.
- **Good to know:** No sign-up. Not on SNAP? Some of these museums also take a WIC card (a different food benefit) or other ID, but they don't have to. Call the museum and ask before you go. [Food Right Now](/safety-and-getting-started/food-right-now/) covers how to apply for SNAP.

:::tip[Your city or county]
Your local **Parks and Recreation** department is the city or county office that runs parks, pools, and community centers. It often has cheap classes for adults, like pottery, painting, dance, or sports. Search for your city or county name plus "parks and recreation activity guide." No internet? Ask at the library desk.
:::

### Take a free class online

**[OpenLearn free courses](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/free-courses)**
Free short online courses from The Open University, a public university in the UK. Beginner choices include "Start writing fiction" and "Introduction to music theory."

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone with internet
- **How to start:** Open the link and pick a course marked "Introductory." You can read without signing up.
- **Good to know:** A free account is optional. It lets you save your progress. The courses are mostly reading.

**[Creativebug](https://www.creativebug.com/libraries)**
Video classes in drawing, painting, sewing, knitting, baking, and more. More than 1,200 libraries pay for it so their card holders can use it for free.

- **Cost:** Free through a library that has it
- **Who can use it:** Anyone with a card from a library that offers it
- **How to start:** On your library's website, look for "Digital Resources" or "eResources" and find Creativebug. Or use "Find a Library Near You" on the Creativebug page.
- **Good to know:** Needs internet and a library card. The main Creativebug site offers a paid trial. You don't need it. Go through your library instead.

## Watch out for

- **Friendly groups that want you to sell.** Some "craft groups" or "wellness communities" are businesses that have you buy a starter kit and recruit friends. These are called multi-level marketing, or MLMs. If a group asks you to pay to join or to recruit others, leave. The [FTC explains the warning signs](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/multi-level-marketing-businesses-and-pyramid-schemes), and [Money Traps to Avoid](/money/money-traps-to-avoid/) has more.
- **Groups that want all of you.** A hobby group meets, does the hobby, and goes home. Before you go, an ordinary group can tell you plainly what it does, what it costs, and when it ends. Be careful with any group that fills your whole week, wants you to drop outside friends, or treats leaving as betrayal. [Staying Safe from People Who Prey on You](/big-life-decisions/staying-safe-from-people-who-prey-on-you/) covers the signs.
- **"Free trials" that ask for a card.** Many hobby and class websites say "free" but ask for your card number. Then they charge you every month if you forget to cancel. Use the free library versions instead.
- **Pressure to sign today.** Some studios and gyms say a deal ends today, or push a long contract on your first visit. That is selling, not teaching. Ask to take the paper home. A place worth joining will let you.
- **Buying the gear first.** Stores will sell a beginner everything. Borrow or buy used until you've done the thing three or four times and still want to.
