## What it is

Building a health record from nothing — finding a doctor, catching up on shots, and getting basic care.

## Why it helps you

If you’ve never had regular care, this is the first step. Free and low-cost clinics exist for exactly this.

## Good to have first

Proof That You Exist (helpful, not always required).

## This opens up

Health Insurance, ongoing care.

## Where to get help

### Find a first doctor

**[Find a Health Center (HRSA)](https://findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov/)**
A free government search tool for community health centers. These clinics give medical and dental care to people of all ages, with or without insurance.

- **Cost:** Based on your income (a "sliding fee scale"). If your income is very low or zero, you get a full discount or pay only a small set fee.
- **Who can use it:** Anyone.
- **How to start:** Type your city or ZIP code. Call the closest clinic and say: "I'm a new patient. I don't have insurance. Can I apply for your sliding fee scale?"
- **Good to know:** The clinic will ask about your income. If you have no pay stubs, ask if you can sign a form that says what you earn. Bring an ID if you have one, but ask what they accept if you don't.

**[Free and charitable clinics (NAFC)](https://nafcclinics.org/find-clinic/)**
A national group of free clinics run by nonprofits and volunteers. Its map helps you find one near you.

- **Cost:** Free or very low cost.
- **Who can use it:** Mostly people with no insurance or very little. Each clinic sets its own rules.
- **How to start:** Search by city or ZIP code. Call the clinic to ask about hours and how to become a patient.
- **Good to know:** Many are open only some days. Some only serve people who live in their county.

### Shots and old records

**[Find your old vaccine records (CDC)](https://www.cdc.gov/iis/contacts-locate-records/index.html)**
Each state keeps a record of shots given there. This CDC page lists who to contact in each state.

- **Cost:** Free.
- **Who can use it:** Anyone looking for their own records.
- **How to start:** Pick the state where you got shots as a child. Follow that state's steps to ask for your record.
- **Good to know:** States usually ask you to prove who you are. If there are no records, that's okay. A doctor can sometimes do a blood test to see if you are already protected. CDC says it is safe to get a shot again if you aren't sure.

**[Your local health department (NACCHO directory)](https://www.naccho.org/membership/lhd-directory)**
Your city or county health department often gives shots, tests, and other basic care. This map finds yours.

- **Cost:** Often free or low cost. Ask first.
- **Who can use it:** People who live in that area.
- **How to start:** Search by state or ZIP code. Call and ask: "Do you have free or low-cost shots for adults without insurance?"
- **Good to know:** They may ask for proof that you live nearby, like a letter with your name and address.

Vaccine advice for adults changed in 2025 and 2026. Rather than going by an old list, ask your clinic or health department which shots are right for you now. CDC's page on [paying for adult vaccines](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines-adults/recommended-vaccines/how-to-pay-adult-vaccines.html) also points people without insurance to health centers and health departments.

### Get help with insurance

**[Find Local Help (HealthCare.gov)](https://www.healthcare.gov/find-local-help/)**
Trained helpers called "assisters" or "navigators" can help you apply for Medicaid or a Marketplace health plan. They are free and don't sell plans.

- **Cost:** Free.
- **Who can use it:** Anyone who needs help getting health coverage.
- **How to start:** Enter your ZIP code to find help in person, by phone, or by email. Or call [1-800-318-2596](tel:18003182596) (TTY 1-855-889-4325), open 24 hours a day.
- **Good to know:** To apply, you'll usually need a Social Security number (if you have one) and a guess at your income. See [Health Insurance and Benefits](/health-and-your-body/health-insurance-and-benefits/) for more.

:::tip[Looking for a therapist or counselor?]
Many therapists offer a short free phone call first. You can ask:

- "What does a visit cost? Do you have a sliding scale?"
- "Do you take my insurance (or Medicaid)?"
- "Can you prescribe medicine, or would I see someone else for that?" (Most counselors can't prescribe.)
- "Do you offer video or phone visits?"

Community health centers often have mental health care too. [FindTreatment.gov](https://findtreatment.gov/) lists mental health and substance use clinics, and you can search it without giving your name.
:::

## If you only do one thing first

Find the community health center closest to you and make a "new patient" visit, even if you feel fine. This gives you a medical home: one place that knows you and keeps your records. It is okay to say, "I don't know my family's health history," or "I've never had a check-up." Staff hear this often, and they will start from where you are.

## Watch out for

- **Plans that look like insurance but aren't.** "Health care sharing ministries" and some "discount plans" are sold online like cheap insurance. They are not insurance and don't have to pay your bills. Ask a free helper at HealthCare.gov before you sign up for anything.
- **Medical credit cards.** Some offices push a credit card for your bill. Many use "deferred interest." If you don't pay it all off by the deadline, you can owe interest all the way back to the start. Ask about the sliding scale or a payment plan with the clinic instead.
- **Paying someone to apply.** Applying for Medicaid, a Marketplace plan, or a clinic's sliding scale is free. If anyone asks for a fee to "speed it up," it is a scam.
- **Using urgent care as your only doctor.** Urgent care is fine for a sudden minor problem. But it often wants payment up front and doesn't follow your health over time. A health center can do both.
