## What it is

The unwritten rules “everyone seems to know” — how restaurants, tipping, and small social moments work, plus a little pop culture to feel in the loop.

## Why it helps you

Knowing the everyday rules helps you relax and belong. You’re learning a new culture, and that’s a normal, doable thing.

## This opens up

Comfort in public, easier friendships and work.

## Where to get help

Some of the best guides to everyday life in the US were written for people moving here from other countries. They explain things from the very beginning, without assuming you already know. That makes them great for anyone who grew up apart from mainstream life.

:::note[Feeling off at first is normal]
People who move into a new culture often go through stages: excitement, then confusion and tiredness, then slowly feeling at home. The middle part is hard, and it passes. Customs also differ by region, age, and community — these are common patterns, not strict rules.
:::

### Guides to everyday life

**[USAHello: Life in the USA](https://usahello.org/life-in-usa/)**
Plain-language articles on American customs, being polite, laws, money, and daily life.

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone
- **How to start:** Click "American culture," then read the page on communication.
- **Good to know:** Written for newcomers and English learners, so it's clear and simple. No account needed.

**[Settle In](https://www.settleinus.org/)**
Short videos and lessons about life in the US, including a section on "cultural adjustment" — getting used to a new culture.

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone
- **How to start:** Go to the website, or get the free "Settle In" app on your phone.
- **Good to know:** Made by the International Rescue Committee for new arrivals, with US government support.

**[LearnFree: Workplace Basics](https://www.learnfree.org/series/workplace-basics)**
Short lessons on first-job basics: new-hire forms, reading your paycheck, and taking time off.

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone
- **How to start:** Open the link and start with lesson 1.
- **Good to know:** LearnFree also has lessons on business communication and email.

**[211](https://www.211.org/)**
A free helpline that can find local life-skills or adult education classes.

- **Cost:** Free
- **Who can use it:** Anyone
- **How to start:** Dial [211](tel:211) and say: "I'm looking for free life-skills or adult education classes near me."
- **Good to know:** Open 24/7.

### A few common customs

:::tip[Small talk]
Small talk is short, light chat that shows you're friendly. "How's your day going?" "Pretty good, thanks — you?" is a full, normal conversation.

- **Easy topics:** weather, food, weekend plans, a show or sport, something nearby ("This coffee is great").
- **Usually saved for close friends:** politics, religion, money (like how much someone earns), weight or looks, and painful past events.
- Many Americans see eye contact as friendly and honest. If it feels hard, looking near someone's eyes is fine.
- People usually stand about an arm's length apart.
:::

:::tip[Tipping]
In the US, a tip is extra money you give some workers. Many restaurant servers are paid low wages and count on tips.

- **Sit-down restaurant (a server brings your food):** usually 15–20% of the bill. Plan for it before you sit down. If money is tight, ordering at a counter or getting take-out is fine.
- **Haircuts, food delivery, taxis and ride apps:** a tip is usually expected.
- **Counter service (coffee, a muffin, take-out):** a tip is optional. When a screen asks for a tip, you can press "No tip." Lots of people do.
- **Never tip** police, government workers, doctors, or teachers. Offering money to an official can look like a bribe.
- A "service charge" on a bill is not always a tip. You can ask, "Does this go to the server?"
:::

:::tip[At work]
- "On time" often means a few minutes early.
- A short, polite email is better than a long one. Try to reply within a day or two.
- Some people shake hands; some prefer a nod or wave. It's fine to follow the other person's lead.
:::

### Catching up on pop culture

You don't need to know every song or show. It's okay to say, "I missed that one — what's it about?" Most people enjoy explaining. Your public library has free movies, music, and magazines you can borrow or use there, and librarians are glad to suggest popular picks.

## If you only do one thing first

Spend 15 minutes on USAHello's American culture pages. Pick one custom that has confused you, and read just that one. Small, steady steps add up.

## Watch out for

- **Over-friendly "business opportunities."** Someone you just met offers a "life-changing opportunity" where you buy products to sell. These schemes (MLMs) usually lose people money. A real job pays you; you don't pay it.
- **Pricey "success" seminars and life coaches.** A free intro class turns into pressure to pay thousands. Real classes at libraries and community colleges are free or low-cost and don't push you.
- **Money requests in support groups.** If someone in a group asks for a loan or an "investment," say no and tell the group leader.
- **Payday loans and check-cashing stores.** They charge very high fees. A credit union is usually a cheaper place to start.
- **Paying for free government forms.** Real government forms are free. Check that the web address ends in .gov.
